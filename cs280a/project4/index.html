Part A · A.2

Recovering Homographies

Method

A correspondence \((x, y) \leftrightarrow (x', y')\) is related by the homography \(H\) in homogeneous coordinates. We fix the last entry of \(H\) to 1, which leaves 8 unknowns.

$$ \begin{bmatrix} w x' \\ w y' \\ w \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_1 & h_2 & h_3 \\ h_4 & h_5 & h_6 \\ h_7 & h_8 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Dividing by \(w\) and rearranging gives two equations per correspondence that are linear in \(h_1, \dots, h_8\).

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -x x' & -y x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -x y' & -y y' \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_1 \\ h_2 \\ h_3 \\ h_4 \\ h_5 \\ h_6 \\ h_7 \\ h_8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \end{bmatrix} $$

Stacking the rows of all \(n\) correspondences gives a linear system, which we solve by least squares.

$$ A h = b, \qquad A \in \mathbb{R}^{2n \times 8}, \quad b \in \mathbb{R}^{2n} $$ $$ \hat h = \arg\min_h \lVert A h - b \rVert^2 = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T b $$

Click errors cause errors in \(H\) but least squares averages out this error. The solution \(\hat h\) is reshaped into \(H\) with 1 as the last entry.

Part A · A.3

Warping the Images

Method

For each output pixel we find its source location in the input image with \(H^{-1}\) and interpolate there. Unlike forward warping, this leaves no holes.

The four input corners mapped through \(H\) give the bounding box of the warped image.

$$ (0, 0), \quad (M-1, 0), \quad (0, N-1), \quad (M-1, N-1) \;\;\xrightarrow{\;H\;}\;\; x_{\min}, x_{\max}, y_{\min}, y_{\max} $$

Output pixel \((u, v)\) maps back to its source location \((x, y)\).

$$ \begin{bmatrix} \tilde x \\ \tilde y \\ \tilde w \end{bmatrix} = H^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} u + x_{\min} \\ v + y_{\min} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad (x, y) = \left( \frac{\tilde x}{\tilde w},\; \frac{\tilde y}{\tilde w} \right) $$

Pixel centers lie at integer coordinates. Output pixels whose source falls outside the input image stay black and get alpha 0.

Nearest neighbor takes the value of the closest pixel.

$$ g(u, v) = f\big(\operatorname{round}(x),\; \operatorname{round}(y)\big) $$

Bilinear blends the four surrounding pixels, weighted by the fractional offsets \(a = x - x_1\) and \(b = y - y_1\), where \(x_1 = \lfloor x \rfloor\), \(y_1 = \lfloor y \rfloor\), \(x_2 = x_1 + 1\) and \(y_2 = y_1 + 1\).

$$ g(u, v) = (1-a)(1-b)\, f_{11} + a(1-b)\, f_{21} + (1-a)\, b\, f_{12} + a\, b\, f_{22}, \qquad f_{ij} = f(x_i, y_j) $$

Rectification

We click the four corners of a square carpet tile and map them to a \(100 \times 100\) square. Warping with the resulting \(H\) gives a top down view of the floor. The output covers only the part of the photo below the floor’s vanishing line, where \(w = 0\). Objects that are not on the floor, such as cabinets and chairs, are stretched.

Nearest Neighbor vs. Bilinear

MethodTime (s)
Nearest neighbor0.05
Bilinear0.13

Nearest neighbor reads one pixel per output pixel and bilinear reads four, so bilinear is about 2.6 times slower. Where the warp stretches the image, nearest neighbor repeats pixels and gives blocky edges, while bilinear gives smooth transitions with slight blur. Both run in well under a second, so we use bilinear for the mosaics.

Part A · A.4

Blending the Images into a Mosaic

Method

We leave one reference image unwarped, boat4 for Set 1, office2 for Set 2 and desk2 for Set 3, and warp the others into its frame with their homographies from A.2.

The canvas covers the reference image and all warped bounding boxes. A translation \(T\) moves its top left corner \((x_{\min}, y_{\min})\) to the origin.

$$ T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -x_{\min} \\ 0 & 1 & -y_{\min} \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \text{image } i \mapsto T H_i, \qquad \text{reference} \mapsto T $$

Each image is weighted by its distance transform \(\alpha_i\), which is 1 at the image center and 0 at its border.

$$ I(q) = \frac{\sum_i \alpha_i(q)\, I_i(q)}{\sum_i \alpha_i(q)} $$

This feathering blurs fine details that are slightly misaligned, so we use two band blending. The low band \(G_\sigma * I_i\) is feathered, and the high band \(I_i - G_\sigma * I_i\) is taken from the image with the largest \(\alpha_i\).

Part A · A.5 · Bells & Whistles

Spherical Mapping

Method

A planar mosaic stretches content far from the reference view, since positions on the plane grow with \(\tan\theta\). We instead project the mosaic onto a sphere around the camera. An output pixel \((u, v)\) corresponds to longitude \(\theta\) and latitude \(\phi\), with \(f\) pixels per radian.

$$ \theta = \frac{u}{f}, \qquad \phi = \frac{v}{f} $$

The direction \((\theta, \phi)\) is the ray \((\sin\theta\cos\phi,\, \sin\phi,\, \cos\theta\cos\phi)\). It meets the reference image plane at the point below, where \((c_x, c_y)\) is the reference image center.

$$ x = f \tan\theta + c_x, \qquad y = \frac{f \tan\phi}{\cos\theta} + c_y $$

We map this point into each original image with \(H_i^{-1}\) and sample it bilinearly. Sampling the original images directly, rather than the warped planar mosaic, keeps the full resolution. The images are blended with the two band blending from A.4. The output covers the angular range of all image borders. For the boat set, \(f = 4370\) pixels follows from the EXIF focal length of 25 mm. For the office and desk sets, which have no focal length metadata, we choose the \(f\) that gives the least distortion.
