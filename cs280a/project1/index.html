CS280A · Project 1

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire around 1907–1915 by exposing three black-and-white glass plates of the same scene through red, green, and blue filters. We reconstruct color by finding the right alignment between the three plates, as the camera and subject shifted slightly between exposures.

Part 1

Single-Scale Alignment

The three low-resolution scans (cathedral.jpg, monastery.jpg, tobolsk.jpg) are small enough to brute-force. We hold the blue channel fixed and exhaustively search every integer displacement (dx, dy) of the green and red channels in a ±15 pixel window, scoring each candidate against a cropped, border-free center region. Two scoring metrics were compared — L2 / sum-of-squared-differences (minimize distance) and normalized cross-correlation (maximize similarity) or NCC — and for all three images they converge on the same displacement.

Part 2

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment (with NCC)

The full-resolution glass plate scans are far too large for an exhaustive ±15 px search — and the true displacement can exceed that window anyway. Instead I build an image pyramid by repeatedly halving each channel (box-filter averaging), align at the coarsest level with a wide search window, then propagate that estimate down the pyramid — doubling it and refining with a small local search at each finer scale — until I'm back at full resolution. All alignment is scored with normalized cross-correlation.

Special case — emir.tif

Plain-intensity NCC pyramid alignment fails on emir.tif: it converges on R (−205, 141), a wild outlier next to every other image's offsets, producing a clearly ghosted double image. Note: I fixed this by aligning on image gradients instead of raw pixel intensity — i.e. computing the score on each channel's gradient magnitude map rather than its intensity values — which is far less sensitive to the flat, saturated fabric colors in this scene that were confusing intensity-based NCC.

Part 3

Bells & Whistles

Automatic border cropping.

The raw scans have a dark plate border and colored fringing around the edges that I wanted cut off automatically. I run a Sobel filter on each channel and sum it along rows and columns.

I take the innermost spike past that threshold, not the tallest one, since the outer seams between plate bands are always sharper than the inner seam against the actual photo. Then I intersect the crop bounds across all three color channels, since each one sits at a slightly different spot on the plate and has its own border.

I used two parameters, cap=0.10, k=5.0. cap is how far in from each edge the detector is allowed to look, and k is the threshold multiplier, thr = k * np.median(edge_prof). So a column only counts as a border candidate if its gradient sum exceeds the k parameter. Some images with large inner variance such as siren, self_portrait, icon, still under-crops slightly. This algorithm fails if there are too much variance.

Automatic contrasting.

Scanned plates rarely use the full intensity range. A plate might span only 0.08 to 0.82, leaving the image flat and hazy. I rescale linearly by subtracting the global minimum and dividing by the global range, I′ = (I − Imin) / (Imax − Imin), so the darkest pixel becomes black and the brightest becomes white. I use a single minimum and maximum across all three channels rather than per-channel values, which preserves the ratios between channels so only contrast changes and colours stay intact. Stretching each channel independently would amplify a weak channel more than a strong one and shift the colour balance. Because the true extremes are used, no clipping is needed, though the method is sensitive to outliers, since a single dust speck can set the maximum and neutralise the stretch. A percentile-based variant would avoid that.

Automatic white balance.

What a plate records is surface reflectance multiplied by the colour of the light, so a white wall under warm light produces orange pixels. White balancing estimates the illuminant and divides it out. Estimating it is the hard part, since only the product is observable, so I implemented the two standard heuristics. Gray world assumes the average reflectance of a scene is neutral, making the per-channel average the illuminant. White world assumes the brightest surface is white, and I use the 99th percentile rather than the true maximum so a single dust speck cannot set the estimate. Correction is then a diagonal transform, scaling each channel by the ratio of the mean illuminant to that channel's illuminant, which keeps overall brightness fixed and changes only the ratios between channels. Since gains above 1 push bright pixels past 1.0, I renormalise by the new maximum rather than clipping, preserving relative values instead of blowing out highlights. Both heuristics rest on assumptions that are often false, and gray world in particular fails on scenes dominated by one colour, such as dense foliage, where the average genuinely is green and the method removes real colour rather than a cast.

Gradient-based alignment.

Aligned on gradient magnitude instead of raw intensity.

def edge_map(im):
    gx = sobel(im, axis=1)
    gy = sobel(im, axis=0)
    return np.sqrt(gx**2 + gy**2)

You compute the edge map of each channel, run the pyramid search on those to get the displacement, then apply that displacement to the original channels. The edge maps are scaffolding and get discarded.

The reason it helps is Emir. His robe is bright blue, so it reads near white in the blue channel and near black in the red. Raw intensity matching sees two very different images and picks a wrong offset. But the outline of the robe, the door frame, and the folds sit in identical places in every channel, because structure is shared even when brightness is not.

Color mapping.

Prokudin-Gorskii's filters were whatever glass and dyes were available in 1907, with overlapping spectral transmission curves that do not correspond to modern sRGB primaries, so each channel carries some contamination from its neighbours even in a perfectly aligned composite. The correction is a 3×3 matrix applied to every pixel, where the diagonal does what white balance already does and the off-diagonal terms undo the filter overlap. I used a one-parameter model motivated by the physics, assuming each channel picks up an equal share of its neighbours, giving a matrix whose rows sum to 1 so neutral grays stay neutral. Positive values remove cross-channel contamination and increase saturation, negative values add it. I swept this parameter and selected by eye, then tuned each channel separately since the overlap between blue and green is physically not the same as between red and blue.

The final values I settled on were (a_r, a_g, a_b) = (0.3, −0.25, 0.1), giving the matrix: 

M = [[ 1.6 , -0.3 , -0.3 ],
     [ 0.25,  0.5 ,  0.25],
     [-0.1 , -0.1 ,  1.2 ]]
The after version is much better balanced in terms of green and red, where the original image was dominated by bright green.

Part 4

Final Results

Every image, including all the modification, processed end to end: multi-scale pyramid alignment (gradient-based for emir.tif), automatic border cropping, automatic contrast, white balance, and the final 3×3 color mapping matrix. "Before" is the plain NCC pyramid alignment; "after" has every bell and whistle applied.

Note: the color mapping matrix is tuned globally, not per-image, so it doesn't always help. On siren.tif it removes too much green. The lilac bush's leaves come out duller and more olive than the plain aligned version, since the matrix was tuned against a scene with a different color balance.
